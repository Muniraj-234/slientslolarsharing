The problem

A solar charging station has a ceiling that moves with the sun. Its users do not.

A 10 kWp array gives about 8 kW at noon, less under cloud and nothing after sunset. Ten e-rickshaws and scooters plugged in together ask for more than 10 kW.

  • The station trips. With nothing limiting the load, the converter or battery protection cuts out and every vehicle stops charging until someone unplugs.
  • The first to arrive takes everything. Whoever plugs in early charges at full power; the driver who arrives sixth gets a trip or nothing.
  • Nobody can plan. A driver who earns by the trip cannot tell whether the wait is ten minutes or two hours.
  • Owners overspend. The usual fix is to oversize panels, battery and converter for a peak that lasts an hour, or to install fewer sockets and turn customers away.
The solution

One controller that treats power as a budget and shares it every second

Silent Assistant sits between the DC bus and the charging ports. It measures what the sun and battery can give, asks each port what its vehicle can take, and sets every port's charging power so the total never exceeds the supply.

Allocates fairly

Max-min sharing with a guaranteed minimum. Small loads get what they need, large loads split the rest equally, priority users (ambulance e-rickshaw, health worker) count double. If power cannot cover every minimum, the least-charged vehicles go first and the order rotates every 10 minutes.

Enforces limits

Per-port cap of 1.5 kW, converter rating of 10 kW, backup feed rating of 3 kW, battery reserve floor at 20%, thermal derating at 70 °C, and a relay that opens when a port draws more than it was told to. While others wait, each session is capped at 3 kWh or 80% charge.

Adapts when users join or leave

The share is recalculated every control cycle and at once on plug-in, unplug, cloud or fault. Cuts are immediate so the limit is never crossed; increases ramp at 0.5 kW per minute so the bus stays stable.

Shows status and waiting time

A kiosk screen shows each port's power, charge level and time left. People in the queue see an estimated wait worked out from the energy still owed to the vehicles ahead of them and the power they are receiving.

Keeps running on backup power

When the battery falls to 30% the controller brings in a 3 kW grid backup feed, adds it to the power budget and shares it like any other source. Solar is always used first, the backup never charges the battery, and it is released when the battery recovers to 45%. If the grid is out, the station carries on with sun and battery alone.

      Control loop, once per second

      1. Read PV power, bus voltage, battery charge and converter temperature.
      2. Work out the budget: the smaller of the converter rating and (solar power + what the battery may give at its present charge + the backup feed when it is engaged).
      3. Switch the backup feed: on when the battery is at or below 30%, off again at 45%.
      4. Read each port: is a vehicle plugged in, how much can it accept, what current is it drawing.
      5. Open the relay on any port drawing more than 120% of its setpoint.
      6. Share the budget with the rule on the right.
      7. Apply setpoints: cut at once, raise by at most 0.5 kW per minute.
      8. Send surplus solar to the battery; update the kiosk, the waiting times and the SCADA telemetry.

      The sharing rule

      // items in admission order: {demand, min, weight}
admit items while their minimums fit in the budget
if (sum of demands <= budget) give everyone full demand
else find level k by bisection so that
  sum( clamp(k * weight, min, demand) ) == budget
setpoint[i] = clamp(k * weight[i], min[i], demand[i])

      Example with 4.0 kW for four e-rickshaws, an e-cycle and a phone locker: the cycle gets its full 0.30, the locker its 0.12, and the rickshaws 0.895 kW each. The same function runs in this page (JavaScript) and in the ESP32 firmware (C++).

      Bench prototype wiring (4 ports, 12 V)

      Each channel: INA219 measures current, a logic-level MOSFET (IRLZ44N, 220 Ω gate resistor, 10 kΩ pull-down) sets power by PWM, a relay isolates the port on a fault. The commercial unit replaces the MOSFET with a CC/CV DC-DC charger module that takes a current setpoint over CAN.

      Run the circuit virtually in Wokwi

      The wokwi folder beside this file holds an ESP32 project: sketch.ino, allocator.h, diagram.json and libraries.txt.

      1. Open wokwi.com and start a new ESP32 project.
      2. Paste sketch.ino and diagram.json over the defaults, add allocator.h as a new file, and add the LiquidCrystal I2C library.
      3. Press play. The potentiometer is the sun. The four slide switches plug vehicles into ports 1 to 4, and the fifth adds a waiting user.
      4. LED brightness is each port's charging power. The LCD shows the budget, each port's share and the waiting time; the red LED lights when sharing is active.

      The serial monitor prints one JSON telemetry line per second, the same message the commercial unit publishes over MQTT to the SCADA server.

      Why a station owner buys

      More sockets on the same solar plant, and no trips

      • Ten ports can run on a plant sized for six, so the owner avoids buying extra panels, battery and converter for a short peak.
      • No overload trips and no lost charging time after each one.
      • The battery never goes below its reserve, which protects the most expensive part that wears out.
      • Drivers see a waiting time and come back.
      • Charging continues after dark on a small 3 kW backup connection, instead of a large grid connection sized for the full load.

      Risks we will be asked about

      • Charger compatibility: power can only be varied on chargers that accept a current setpoint. For plain AC sockets the controller falls back to switching ports in timed turns.
      • Subsidy access: central charging subsidies flow through state agencies, not to a startup directly.
      • Backup dependence: the backup feed needs a grid connection. Where the grid is unreliable the station still runs on sun and battery, with shorter evening hours.
      • Price sensitivity: the payback above depends on utilisation; a station used half as much takes twice as long.
